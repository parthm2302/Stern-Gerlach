Why the beam splits

An atom with a magnetic moment acts like a tiny compass needle. In a field that gets stronger towards one pole, the atom is pushed up or down depending on how its moment lines up with the field.

If the moment could point in any direction, the atoms would spread into a smear. They don't. The component along the field only takes a few allowed values, so the beam splits into separate paths.

Silver atoms work well because their magnetic moment comes from the spin of a single outer electron.

About this simulation: each atom's result is picked at random with the probabilities shown. Real beams also have a spread of speeds and imperfect fields, which this model leaves out. The apparatus is always drawn with the deflection vertical, whichever axis you choose.

Fz = μz · ∂Bz/∂z

The push on an atom depends on its magnetic moment along the field. A bigger moment component means a bigger deflection.

Sz = mħ, with m = −s, −s+1, …, +s

A particle with spin s has 2s + 1 allowed values of m, so it makes 2s + 1 beams. Spin 1/2 makes two.

P(up) = cos²(γ/2)

γ is the angle between the incoming spin and the field axis. At 0° every atom goes up. At 90° it is a 50/50 split.

P(m) = C(2s, s+m) · ps+m (1−p)s−m

For higher spin, with p = cos²(γ/2), the outcomes follow a binomial distribution. A random, unpolarised beam gives every outcome equal weight.

How the result was understood

The split silver deposit was first read as support for the older quantum theory of the atom. The correct explanation, electron spin, came a few years later.

1922

Stern and Gerlach send a beam of silver atoms through an uneven magnetic field in Frankfurt. The deposit on the detector plate splits in two.

1925

George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit propose that the electron has an intrinsic spin.

1927

T. E. Phipps and J. B. Taylor repeat the experiment with hydrogen atoms. Their ground state has no orbital motion, so the splitting must come from spin.

1943

Stern receives the Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on molecular beams and the magnetic moment of the proton.
